### A2. Years, number built, survivors

- **Design start:** BANKA says design studies began "in Czechoslovakia in 1976" and the Stratos was "conceived in 1982". WIKI says design work resumed in May 1983. HIST says late 1983.
- **Prototype S1 first flight:** 25 April 1985 at Penfield, flown by Ligeti, 45 min (BANKA: "it made a 45 minute first flight on 25 April 1985"). In 1986 it flew at Oshkosh, the Seattle air fair and Expo 86 in Vancouver (BANKA, AEROP).
- **S1 hours:** ATSB87: "The prototype version had successfully flown some 340 hours." (P/R)
- **Orders:** BANKA: "By that time 200 orders had been placed". AEROP: "In April 1986 a production run of 20 aircraft was commenced". (F, as reported secondhand)
- **Airframes that flew, per the official record:** at least three.
  1. S1 prototype (about 340 h).
  2. "Production version" (S2), destroyed on its first flight on 22 Sep 1987 (ATSB87).
  3. A Stratos destroyed on its "first test flight" at Mangalore VIC on 10 Jul 1988, pilot seriously injured, "Not Registered" (ATSB88). This cannot be S1, which already had about 340 h. **The pilot's name and the airframe's identity are not found.**
- **Number built:** WIKI infobox gives "3". NOD (2004) lists "three know versions": the prototype, the crashed production model, and Ron Ligeti's new build (unfinished). AEROP says "three examples were built" but its account is internally inconsistent: it attributes the 340 h to the crashed aircraft and contradicts ATSB88 (below). **The exact number is unresolved.**
- **Survivors:**
  - NOD quotes Ron Ligeti (B, c. 2004) on the prototype: "it is still in airworthy condition and is the only one in existence".
  - BANKA (2010): "The original prototype was still airworthy and in the hands of the Ligeti family in 2006".
  - AEROP photo caption: "Restored Ligeti Stratos at Temora, NSW in 2012".
  - AEROP also says the Mangalore aircraft was "restored to static display standard at Holbrook for the Australian Ultralight Museum. This aircraft 19-7441 (c/n S3-001)". ATSB88 records that aircraft as "Destroyed".
  - A forum post (kasper, 8 Jan 2017) says "The one in the museum is the original and flew quite well" (https://www.aircraftpilots.com/forums/topic/28504-rohr-2-175-ducted-prop-aircraft).
  - **Which airframe is at Holbrook (S1 or S3-001) is unresolved.** Ron Ligeti's new build is reported stopped. NOD: "(anno 2024) Sadly i heard that Ron stopped working on the new edition."

## Part B: the crash that killed Charles Ligeti

**Official record:** ATSB (then BASI) Aviation Safety Investigation Report **198701462**, "Ligeti Stratos, 1 km SE Penfield VIC, 22 September 1987", approved for release 17 June 1988.
PDF: https://www.atsb.gov.au/sites/default/files/investigation-reports/aair198701462.pdf · page: https://www.atsb.gov.au/investigations/198701462
(The `.../media/34032/aair198701462.pdf` URL that search engines show now returns 404.)

| Field | Record (R) |
|---|---|
| Date / time | 22 September 1987, 1750 (departed 1730) |
| Place | 1 km SE Penfield VIC |
| Aircraft | Ligeti Stratos, manufacturer Ligeti Aero-Nautical Pty Ltd |
| Registration | "Not Registered"; serial "N/A" |
| Operation | "Sport Aviation (Test Flight)" |
| Injury / damage | 1 fatal (crew); destroyed |
| Category | Loss of control |

**What the record says happened** (verbatim):
- "This aircraft was intended to be the production version of the "Stratos" aircraft."
- "These changes included the removal of the dihedral from the main wing and the use of full span elevators on the canard wing and full span ailerons on the main wing. The engine mounting was lowered such that the ducted propeller was totally below the main wing and the lower part of the propeller duct was extended well forward to form a "channel or strake"."
- "As far as the investigation could determine, the effect of these modifications had not been checked by wind tunnel or other methods prior to this flight."
- "The preparation included a determination of the centre of gravity, although no record was kept of these calculations."
- "Witnesses reported that the aircraft then carried out a very slow pass over the aerodrome at a height of between 400 and 500 feet above ground level."
- "It is uncertain whether the aircraft suddenly pitched nose up or nose down, but all witnesses agreed that it then fell vertically while the nose swung in a pendulous motion. The aircraft struck the ground in an inverted attitude with little or no horizontal speed."
- "there was no indication of any airframe or control failure prior to ground impact. The engine was test run and strip inspected and no fault could be found."

**Cause analysis in the record** (verbatim):
- "it is possible that the modifications incorporated in the new aircraft had an adverse effect on the stall characteristics. Analysis of the modifications indicates that the most significant effect on the stall characteristics would have resulted from use of full span elevators on the canard wing."
- "The result is that the canard would be developing more lift, compared to the same wing without full span elevators, before the wing stalled and the stall would be sharper. Because the main wing is still producing lift at this stage the nose down pitch following the stall of the canard would be aggravated. It is considered likely that the accident sequence was initiated by a full canard stall followed by a rapid nose down pitch."

**Significant factors** (verbatim): "1. The pilot encountered unforeseen circumstances beyond his capabilities. 2. The aircraft was operated at too low an altitude, on a test flight, to enable recovery from any unexpected divergences from normal flight." It then adds: "Because the investigation was not able to positively determine all the reasons for the accident, the following possible factors are also considered appropriate. 1. The pre-test flight preparation was inadequate in that no apparent attempt had been made to check the performance of the aircraft following the significant design changes made to the prototype prior to this flight. 2. The pilot may have been suffering some fatigue following a full day preparing the aircraft for flight."

**Plain statement: did the configuration contribute?** The official record does **not** blame the closed/joined-wing configuration as such. It does not list the configuration as a factor. It contrasts the S2 with the S1 prototype, which "appeared to suffer no adverse flying characteristics" over about 340 h. The record attributes the loss "likely" to **design changes made to the production airframe**, chiefly full-span canard elevators that sharpened the canard stall and aggravated the nose-down pitch. Other factors were the untested modifications, the unrecorded CG, and test-flying too low to recover. The stall mechanism itself was not positively determined: the record says "not able to positively determine all the reasons", and witnesses could not agree whether the nose went up or down. Classification: **configuration not cited; the specific canard-control modification was the likely cause; the root mechanism was not positively determined.** The analysis is canard-type (front-surface stall with the rear surface still lifting). It applies to any canard or tandem layout and is not unique to joined tips.

**Speculation, kept apart from the record:**
- Dave Limmer (a Facebook comment reproduced on NOD, https://nestofdragons.net/weird-airplanes/tandemwings/ligeti-stratos/info-about-stratos-on-internet/): "I might be recalling incorrectly but I believe that the front wing stalled first as expected, but with the new configuration, the nose dropped excessively, rather than just nodding." He labels it as recollection.
- Ron Ligeti (B, via NOD): "The version that did crash (1987) was a production model that employed a highly experimental - high lift devise as well as other major aerodynamic changes that changed it's stability."
- HIST (family): S2 "would have a much more pronounced stall than the S1 ... and would need more altitude to effect recovery", and Ron believes "Charles's conservative approach to flight testing was compromised ... due to business pressures".
- Forum (enthalpic, 2009, homebuiltairplanes): "Consensus opinion is that he got lucky with the original in terms of stability but no2 was different".
- BANKA says the aircraft "stalled during approach to landing". The ATSB says only that control was lost "shortly after the turn" back toward the strip.

**Second Stratos accident (relevant to the configuration):** ATSB **198801418**, Ligeti Stratos, Mangalore VIC, 10 July 1988, serious injury, destroyed, "Sport Aviation (Test Flight)", the aircraft's first test flight. The cause was engine power loss: "Binding in a Bowden cable probably prevented the enrichment valve from de-activating" and "A loose vacuum pipe probably resulted in engine fuel starvation followed by complete loss in engine power." Factor 5: "There was a lack of elevator response as the pilot attempted a landing after the engine lost power." The pilot's report of pitch stability going "almost neutral" when power was lost, and the nose dropping on throttle closure, point to a strong power-pitch coupling in the lowered-duct layout. That is my reading (E) and is not a finding of the record.

## Part D: calibration points for joined/box wings

### D1. Theory

**Prandtl 1924, NACA TN 182 "Induced drag of multiplanes"** (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19930080964; PDF https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930080964/downloads/19930080964.pdf).
- Quote (p. 17 of the TN, read from the page image): "This wing system has the least induced drag of all wing systems of like span and the same total height (sum of the individual gaps)."
- Approximate formula, eq. (13): D/D_mono ≈ (1 + 0.45 G/b) / (1.04 + 2.81 G/b).
- Table III, "d) Best wing system", κ = D/D_I, with my e = 1/κ in brackets (E: arithmetic only):

| G/b | 0.05 | 0.10 | 0.15 | 0.20 | 0.25 | 0.30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Best wing system κ | 0.865 [1.16] | 0.787 [1.27] | 0.728 [1.37] | 0.678 [1.47] | 0.637 [1.57] | 0.601 [1.66] |
| Biplane κ | 0.890 | 0.827 | 0.779 | 0.742 [1.35] | 0.710 | 0.684 |

- **Stratos at G/b ≈ 0.11 (E):** κ ≈ 0.775, so ideal e ≈ 1.29. That is the theoretical ceiling for optimal loading. The Stratos front view is a box (vertical tip fins), not a diamond.

**Kroo 1996, "Highly nonplanar lifting systems"** (NASA NTRS 19960023622, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19960023622; PDF https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19960023622/downloads/19960023622.pdf).
- Figure "Span Efficiency of Various Nonplanar Shapes Height / Span = 0.2" (p. 346, read from the page image): box 1.46, C-wing 1.45, biplane 1.36, **diamond (joined wing in front view) 1.05**, V-dihedral 1.03.
- Quote: "Note the relatively small gain for the diamond-shaped device and the wing with dihedral, while the C-wing shape achieves essentially the same drag as the boxplane."
- Costs (p. 338, about the Lockheed transonic box-plane transport specifically, not box or joined wings in general): "Fuel volume, landing gear integration, CLmax penalties, and lower section Reynolds numbers are some of the disadvantages for this concept."
- On wetted area: "adding vertical surfaces such as winglets add wetted area and weight due to higher bending moments, while the weight of a cantilevered biplane is increased since for a fixed total area, the chords (and dimensional thickness) of each wing are halved."
- On joined wings: "The tail is then in compression, reducing wing bending moments."
- **So the ~1.46 figure for h/b = 0.2 applies to a box. A Wolkovitch diamond joined wing gets only about 1.05 at the same height.** That distinction matters for any claim we make.

**Wolkovitch, "The joined wing: An overview"** (AIAA 85-0274; J. Aircraft 23(3), 1986; NTRS 19850037475 and 19860043821, abstract only).
- Abstract: "Wind-tunnel tests and finite-element structural analyses have shown that the joined wing provides the following advantages over a comparable wing-plus-tail system; lighter weight and higher stiffness, higher span-efficiency factor, higher trimmed maximum lift coefficient, lower wave drag, plus built-in direct lift and direct sideforce control capability." (F: the proponent's claim)

**Costs, from the cited literature:**
- *Aft-wing buckling.* Gallman, Smith & Kroo, AIAA 90-3197 (NTRS 19900062047), abstract: "The structural design algorithm included the effects of secondary bending moments to investigate the possibility of tail buckling and to design joined wings resistant to buckling." Its results: "joint location at about 70 percent of the wing semispan", "save 1.7 percent in direct operating cost and 11 percent in drag". Gallman & Kroo, AIAA 92-4761 (NTRS 19930036359), sized members against "1020 stress constraints and five buckling constraints".
- *Weight drivers.* Miura, Shyu & Wolkovitch, J. Aircraft 1988 (NTRS 19890035446), abstract: "The lightest wings were obtained by increasing dihedral and taper ratio, decreasing sweep and span, increasing fraction of airfoil chord occupied by structural box, and locating the joint inboard of the front wing tip."
- *Stall / lateral stability.* Smith & Stonum, NASA TM-101083 (1989) (https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19890014914), wind-tunnel results on the 1/6-scale JW-1 (the NASA/ACA AD-1 conversion; "never built or flown" withdrawn after the audit, as neither cited source says it):
  - "A maximum lift coefficient" ... "of 1.20 was obtained at a 6* angle of attack" [CL,max; 6°; PDF text layer garbles the subscript], on "a wing with 30°of sweep".
  - "The JW-1 exhibited classical swept-wing stall characteristics where the aircraft was unstable at the stall angle, and became stable again at a high, post-stall angle of attack of, in this case, about 12 °."
  - "Figure 18 shows an unusual loss of lateral stability at high angles of attack. It appears that the positive dihedral contribution from the forward wing was reduced as the wing stalled and the negative dihedral of the rear wing began to dominate."
  - "The vortilons produced a profound improvement in the stall and post-stall characteristics with no measurable effects on cruise performance."
- *Pitch control and CG.* No single citation quantifying the CG range of a joined or box wing was found. IDINTOS (below) uses opposed elevators on both wings for pitch. ATSB87 shows that loading the front surface uniformly makes the stall sharper.

## Biggest gaps

1. **No independent flight test or pilot report.** All Stratos performance numbers are designer/Jane's claims (F). The Sport Aviation (Dec 1986?) feature and Jane's 1987-88 pp. 540-541 text could not be accessed.
2. **Each wing's area and airfoil** are not found. Area split, chords, gap and stagger here are my estimates from a 2003 third-party 3-view, not factory data.
3. **Designer's birth date** is not found, and the Hungarian-born lead is not confirmed (Czechoslovak, Košice per one French page).
4. **Airframe identity and survivors are unresolved:** S1 vs S3-001 (19-7441) at Holbrook, and who flew the 1988 Mangalore aircraft.
5. **Open source:** no licence text found. The original project sites are dead or hijacked. The audit's Wayback retry checked the 2006-2013 snapshots and found no licence named. A 2012 objectives page states an intent to "Distribute the plans gratis; at no charge". A 2014 LGT Aerospace PDF by Ron Ligeti (https://web.archive.org/web/20140826172206/http://lgtaerospace.com/attachments/article/15/Stratos%20Project.pdf, SHA-256 35180b15...ce728b0, re-fetched by AERO) says "The design owner retains the IP" and aims "to induce a licensing arrangement for production". It also says "The only original successful Stratos aircraft is currently in Storage, where it has been for 20 years." A 2010 forum post (aircraftpilots topic 6308, paddo2002) says "the S1 is at Holbrook" (hearsay).
6. **Calibration:** no full-scale flight data for any PrandtlPlane ultralight, and no accident record set for the Sunny. Synergy's full-scale first flight is unconfirmed.
