## Why it died

Anderson Greenwood took control of a struggling Bellanca to build the Aries at Alexandria MN. The Viking plant was nearly idle in 1977 and A-G still had to raise production capital (AOPA 1977). Bellanca went into receivership in 1980 (Plane & Pilot), reportedly drained by the Skyrocket II program. Only about 4 flying airframes were completed. No safety or technical failure is recorded.

## Idea ledger (what a new airplane should keep; AERO's judgement)

- A laminar, constant-chord, flush-riveted metal wing on a fuselage built from flat and single-curve skins to cut production cost: speed (181 KTAS at 6,500 ft on 73% of 250 hp, AOPA 1977) from a design meant to be cheap to build.
- A 250 hp, 1,300 lb useful, four-to-five-seat airframe that is a direct AM20T-class fit; its type certificate (A11SW) is still current and apparently dormant.
- Lesson: it died with its producer (Bellanca's 1980 receivership), not on a technical or safety failure; one NTSB record in about 50 years, on four aircraft.

## Rights and provenance (known so far; research, not legal advice)

TCDS A11SW (Current, Rev 2 1980): holder Anderson, Greenwood and Company, Houston, former holder Bellanca. The A-G valve business later passed via Keystone/Tyco/Pentair to Emerson (secondary sources, unverified), so the TC may sit dormant with a valve company. Owned by the stage 2 rights research (AER-3787).

## Safety record

One NTSB record in about 50 years: FTW83LA173 (1983, N51AG prototype), a taxi brake failure with gear collapse and no injuries. AOPA test noted narrow gear track and weak yaw damping.

### Verdict

**Rank 1.** As published it passes all three floors: 1,300 lb useful, 1,017 nm at economy cruise with a 45-minute reserve, and 181 KTAS at 75% (altitude not stated); AOPA measured 181 KTAS at 6,500 ft on 73% in 1977. Our check agrees with the published speed (drag factor 1.26-1.34 on AOPA's two points, inside the fleet band), so the airframe is as clean as claimed. It reads the published range about 10% optimistic: 1,008 nm still air with no reserve at 65% and 8,000 ft on 76 gal. On an AM20T it is a direct fit (250 hp to 260 hp take-off, similar cruise power): 185-197 KTAS, 1,300 lb useful (factory; no fleet figure exists for four aircraft), 906-919 nm on full fuel at continuous power, and over 1,000 nm at economy power (154-161 KTAS, 859-861 lb with a reserve, as information). Its record is clean (one NTSB event in about 50 years, on four aircraft), it died with its producer rather than for any flaw, it was designed from flat and single-curve skins to be cheap to build, and its type certificate is still current. Its weaknesses: only four exist, so there is no fleet record to judge, and range at continuous power is about 90 nm short on standard fuel.

### The deep-dive axes: storage, home-shop construction, safety, ride

**Aspect ratio 5.8**: a moderate, constant-chord wing, on the conventional side of the planform study (AER-3807, `docs/studies/planform/`).

- **Storage.** 31.3 ft span, 8 ft 7 in high (AOPA): fits a 40 ft T-hangar door with room to spare. No folding; not trailerable without removing the wings.
- **Construction with home-shop tools.** This is its strongest axis. The wing is constant-chord, so every rib is the same: one set of CNC-routed form blocks makes them all. The skins are flat or single-curve aluminium, designed that way to cut production cost; blanks can be routed, but bending them needs a brake and the riveting is hand work. The largest part is a 15.7 ft half-wing; the root spar is about 7.8 in deep, a machined or built-up metal spar that may need a service. Fittings suit a small CNC mill.
- **Safety.** Published stalls 63 kt clean and 56 kt in landing configuration (72 and 64 mph); AOPA measured 69 and 63 mph IAS. AOPA described no stall break, so stall character is unrecorded; it did note weak yaw damping after a rudder input. Best L/D about 15.7 at 114 KTAS. Spin behaviour: not on record. One NTSB event in about 50 years, on four aircraft, proves little.
- **Ride comfort.** Gust dn 1.66: the roughest of the shortlist with the Meyers, because its 18.5 lb/ft2 wing loading is low. Cabin 45 in wide, 115 in long, 48.5 in high (AOPA): roomy for the class. Noise: not recorded.

### Engine fit with the Aeromomentum line

- **Power.** The AM20T (260 hp take-off, 195 hp assumed continuous) replaces a 250 hp O-540 that cruises at 163-188 hp (65-75%). The cruise power is about the same, so the Aries keeps its speed: 185 KTAS at 8,000 ft (low case) and 197 KTAS at 16,000 ft (high case).
- **Mass and CG.** The installed mass falls by about 64 lb (E), in the nose. That needs ballast or a longer mount; the headline useful load takes no credit for the saving.
- **Cooling.** The O-540 used a single inlet below the spinner and no cowl flaps. A radiator needs its own duct, not yet designed; Raymer's cooling drag is carried unchanged.
- **Fuel.** At continuous power the 76 gal usable gives 906-919 nm still air. A modest tank increase, or economy cruise, closes the range floor.

## Stage 4: what modern manufacturing changes (AER-3784)

Full study (rank 1). Method, prices and sources are in [docs/MANUFACTURING.md](../MANUFACTURING.md); the weight table is generated by `docs/manufacturing/weights.py`. Desk study: nothing weighed or quoted by us, nobody contacted. Every E is our estimate.

**Original construction:** certified riveted aluminium, built to be cheap: flat and single-curve skins and a flush-riveted, laminar, constant-chord wing. About four were completed. Drawings and jigs sit with a dormant TC holder; a revival is a re-derivation unless the rights route recovers them.

**Modern route:** a matched-hole CNC aluminium kit (the Van's model). The design's own choice of flat and single-curve skins means no compound molds or stretch-forming. Composite molds only for the cowl, radiator duct, tips and fairings.

**First, what to delete:** carburettor, magnetos, carb heat and mixture control (EFI); the single air-cooled inlet and its baffles (radiator in a duct); 1977 avionics and vacuum system (EFIS).

**Constant chord, one rib:** every wing rib is the same shape, so one form block makes them all, and the single-curve skins are cut flat and rolled. That collapses the wing's unique-part count, which drives both kit tooling and builder hours. It is why the Aries's tooling is the lowest on the list, and why extra fuel bays need no new tooling.

**Empty weight:** rebuild **1,780-2,000 lb**, useful load **1,150-1,370 lb** (factory: 1,300). With our engine credit (-81 to -63 lb; O-540-A4D5 375 lb per TCDS E-295, without starter and alternator): 1,690-1,940 lb. About 20 gal more fuel in integral wing bays closes the 90 nm range shortfall at continuous power, for 10-20 lb empty (option, below).

**Home-shop route:** SendCutSend offers 2024-T3 at 0.025 in only. OSH Cut lists it at 0.032-0.19 in for laser cutting, parts up to 119 x 47 in, and no bending ([material page](https://www.oshcut.com/materialdetails/aluminum-2024-t3), read 2026-10-26, [archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20261026040951/https://www.oshcut.com/materialdetails/aluminum-2024-t3)). Stage 4 said no service cut 0.032-0.040 in, and AER-3868 found nothing at 0.032 in; the material page corrects both. So the home router is a choice, not the only route. On its 4 ft bed the 6 ft wing-skin wrap needs a chordwise splice. OSH Cut's 119 in takes the wrap whole, but a 15.7 ft half-span skin needs a spanwise splice either way. Home-cut costs +600-1,200 h over a service-cut kit, and saves about $40-90k against one, once someone publishes the CAD files.
